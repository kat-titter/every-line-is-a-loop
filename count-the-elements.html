A new periodic table for network biology

codedatasupplement

In short

Chemistry’s periodic table orders the elements, and its gaps once predicted new ones. This essay tries the same trick on the circuits that run a cell, counting their feedback loops instead of electrons, and tests the table on 640 published models. One band of the table turns out full and the other nearly empty, and the empty band is where to look next.

1the puzzle

Same parts, different machines

In January 2000, Nature printed two synthetic circuits back to back. Elowitz and Leibler wired three repressors into a ring in Escherichia coli, the repressilator, and the cells oscillated with periods of hours [1]. Gardner, Cantor and Collins wired two repressors to shut each other off, and their cells held either of two states until a brief pulse flipped them [2]. Same kind of parts, same bacterium. One circuit kept time; the other remembered. The difference was in how the arrows closed.

Chemistry met a puzzle like this in the 1860s: dozens of elements and no order. Mendeleev’s 1869 table gave them one, left gaps, and by 1871 had predicted the weight, density and oxide of one missing element. In 1875 gallium turned up to match, with scandium and germanium behind it [3]. Moseley pinned the order to X-ray spectra in 1913 [4]; electron shells explained the row lengths later. Order, then prediction, then explanation.

Could the circuits in a cell have a table like that? An earlier essay, Every line is a loop, proposes one built on three counts. This essay tests it: does the order hold, and do the gaps predict anything?

2what a loop can do

Flip the arrows and watch

A model of a circuit is a set of equations: for each molecule, how fast it is made and removed. Draw an arrow from one molecule to another when the first changes how fast the second is made or removed. The arrow amplifies (+, drawn →) if more of the first means more of the second, and brakes (−, drawn ⊣) if it means less. A loop is a path of arrows that comes back to where it began. A microphone pointed at its own speaker is an amplifying loop; a thermostat is a braking one. Biologists call them positive and negative feedback.

Now play. The ring below is the repressilator’s wiring, simulated live: TetR, LacI and cI, each acting on the next. Flip its arrows between amplify and brake, and count the brakes each time it ticks.

With one brake or three, it ticks. With none or two, it settles into one of two states: a switch. Signs multiply round a loop, so two brakes cancel like a double negative, and a loop brakes only when it holds an odd number of them. René Thomas proposed the rule decades ago, holding two states needs an amplifying loop and keeping a rhythm needs a braking one [5], and it is now a theorem [6, 7, 8]. The idea behind the proof is the one you just saw: to hold two states, a push must feed back on itself and grow; to keep swinging, something must eventually push back.

Cells build both kinds. The clock in your own cells is a braking loop: the proteins CLOCK and BMAL1 switch on the Per and Cry genes, the PER and CRY proteins build up, move into the nucleus and switch CLOCK and BMAL1 off, and the round trip takes about a day [9]. The lac operon of E. coli is an amplifying loop made of two brakes: lactose disables the repressor LacI, LacI blocks the lac genes, and one of those genes makes the pump that brings lactose in. Two brakes cancel, and cells can hold either state, on or off [10].

A loop can do a third thing. Slide the hill below and watch two cruise controls. One sags as the road steepens. The other gets back to 60, exactly, every time.

The one that recovers keeps a running total of its shortfall and pushes until the total stops growing. A molecule can do the same if its rate of change doesn’t depend on its own level, so it adds up what flows in and out. Put one inside a braking loop and a cell returns exactly to its set point after a push. Bacteria steer this way: the receptor that senses an attractant carries methyl groups, one enzyme adds them steadily and another, switched on by the receptor’s activity, removes them, so the methyl count is a running total and the cell returns exactly to its resting behaviour [11, 12]. Yeast handle salt the same way [13], and a running total can be built from two molecules that destroy each other [14].

So a circuit has three counts: how many loops, how many of them brake, and how many keep a running total. That is the whole idea of the table.

3the table

Three counts make a periodic table

Take a circuit with one loop. The loop brakes or it doesn’t; it keeps a running total or it doesn’t. That is 2 × 2 = 4 kinds. With two loops, each count runs from 0 to 2: 3 × 3 = 9. With three, 4 × 4 = 16.

down: loops through a running total (j)

Each kind is an element. If 4, 9, 16 rings a bell, it is the number of orbitals in an atom’s second, third and fourth shells, and the match runs deeper. Sort a row by l = max(k, j), the larger of its two counts, and it splits into layers of 1, 3, 5 and 7, the sizes of the s, p, d and f subshells; inside a layer, m = k − j runs from −l to l, as the magnetic number does:

(n+1)2=∑l=0n(2l+1)

It is a counting identity, not physics, and it stops one step short: an atom’s shells hold twice as many electrons because each orbital takes two, and the earlier essay reads that missing factor as a missing binary, perhaps the order in which loops nest. Periodicity is itself a loop. The first periodic system wound the elements along a helix on a cylinder [15], and drawn as spirals, one turn per row, both tables line their families up on rays.

Others had sorted circuits by their loops before: oscillators [16], the named small circuits [17], the wiring patterns that recur across organisms [18, 19]. The table puts the three counts together, much as Ahnert and colleagues built a periodic table for protein complexes [20]. Here it is. Point at an element to read it.

If this is a real periodic table, three things should be true: circuits with a given number of loops do no more kinds of things than their row has elements; no circuit does what its loops forbid, no switch without an amplifying loop and no clock without a brake; and every element can be built, so each blank is a prediction. The second is the theorem above; the other two are open. To test them, count real circuits.

4the census

Counting real circuits

BioModels is a public library of the mathematical models behind published papers, founded in 2005 [21], each one checked against its paper and stored in a standard format [22, 23]: the cell cycle, the fruit fly’s day clock, calcium spikes and more. The census took the 640 models of the 2017 release, the one Baudier, Fages and Soliman analysed, so the counts can be compared [24]. It drew each model’s arrows, listed its loops, dropped the loops that chemistry makes for free (binding a partner and letting go is out and back, not round; Supplement B), and placed the circuit on the table. Click a step to see what it kept, then search the models yourself.

5what the table says

One band full, one band empty

Of the 613 models the census could read, 175 have one to three loops, few enough to place. Look back at Figure 5 before reading on, and notice where the blanks are.

They fall in one band. Every element without a running total is filled, all 10 of them, and familiar circuits sit where their biology puts them: the repressilator under one brake, the trp operon under two. Of the 20 elements with a running total, 3 are filled, by 4 models between them. The table is fullest where biology already had words for what it saw, switches and clocks, and emptiest where the words arrived last.

The three tests are scored below; the weak one is the third, where a strict reading of the equations fills 5 of 9 elements at two loops, a looser one fills 9, and even in yeast, where experiments pinned the running total down [13], neither finds it cleanly.

One surprise. The fruit fly’s day clock has five curated models, all built from the same proteins, and they land in four different places, from one to 14 loops. Measure a coastline with a shorter ruler and it gets longer; a loop count grows the same way as a model gains detail (Supplement C). And one correction: an audit of the first run found a bug that had hidden loops in 42 models; fixing it moved the count from 179 to 175 (Supplement B).

6the empty band

What the gaps say

Mendeleev’s gaps came with predictions, and so do these. Element 2 (Sw∫) is a switch that keeps a running total. It should add up a signal and commit once the total is high enough: a switch that flips on how long something lasted rather than how strong it was. It is this table’s eka-aluminium, and nothing in the census sits there yet.

Why is the band empty? Three readings, each with its own test. The equations may hide them: the strict and the loose reading disagree across most of the band, and the circuits where experiments found a running total, chemotaxis and yeast osmotic stress [12, 13], are too tangled for this census to place; if so, better readings, tested on models with known answers, will fill the band. Nobody may have modelled them yet: running totals entered the field’s vocabulary in 2000 [12] and were built as synthetic parts by 2019 [14, 25], while the release counted here dates from 2017; if so, newer releases will fill the band. Or cells rarely build them small: an exact running total needs special chemistry, such as two molecules that destroy each other [14], so cells may keep theirs inside larger networks; if so, computer-built circuits will find these elements working [26] while models of real cells keep them rare.

What does an empty band say about the hypothesis itself? Taken at face value, that the table is two thirds proven and one third promised. Loops and brakes are real axes: circuits spread across every combination of them. Running totals are, so far, an axis with almost no one on it. Mendeleev’s table carried predictions that never came true as well [3], and if this band stays empty after better readings and newer releases, the honest move is his: retire the axis, and each row shrinks from (loops + 1)2 elements to loops + 1, which is 2, 3 and 4. If the band fills, the square count stands. Either outcome is a result.

The census can’t yet tell these apart, but it does say where to look: models with a known running total, reduced to a few loops; releases after 2017; other collections such as Cell Collective and the Physiome Model Repository; and decisions that hinge on how long a signal lasts, where Sw∫ should live.

7next()

Science is a loop: what comes next?

The census can’t falsify the count itself, any more than a die can roll a seven; what it can do is close one turn of the research loop. Click a stage to see this round and the next.

First, a count that holds still. Proton number doesn’t depend on how you draw an atom; loop number depends on how you draw a circuit, so the table needs a count taken on a reduced model, with fast binding steps merged. If it works, the five fly-clock models should land in one element. Then the first claim gets a proper test by building every small circuit on a computer and running it across its rates [26, 27], and the blanks become predictions: the running-total circuit built in bacteria in 2019 [14, 25, 28] belongs in element 3, the adapter (Ad).

Then the part chemistry’s table is remembered for. Mendeleev’s eka-silicon became germanium, and germanium became the first transistor [29]. His eka-manganese, element 43, was made before it was found, in 1937 [30]; as technetium it became the workhorse of medical imaging. A table that predicts elements ends up predicting materials and medicines.

For cells, the same three gifts. For the field, a shared address: a model says which element it is, a reader asks whether its behaviour matches, and circuits built from different molecules in different organisms can turn out to be the same element, as the fly’s clock and ours are at their coarsest drawing, one braking loop through transcription.

For medicine, a diagnosis by element. The earlier essay reads three diseases as one algebra: a tumour as a brake lost, an autoimmune disease as a brake missing, a prion-like disease as an amplifying loop with no brake at all. The table turns that into a target, which loop to put back rather than which molecule to hit, and the clinic already has cases. Restore p53, a brake, and established tumours in mice regress [31, 32]. Remove a brake on T cells, as checkpoint blockade does, and they attack tumours and the body alike [33]: the price the table puts on a lost brake. An element also says what to measure in a patient: a clock by its period, a switch by its threshold, a running total by its drift.

For technology, design by position, as a chemist who needs a reactive metal looks in the first column. A sensor that must reset exactly comes from Ad, where the circuit built in bacteria is the first part in the box and the closed-loop insulin pump is the same element built outside the cell. A timer that commits after a dose comes from Sw∫, which nobody has built. Wire a toggle switch to a running total and drive it with pulses of different lengths; if it flips on the total rather than the peak, Sw∫ is made before it is found, as element 43 was.

8limits

What this census can’t tell you

  • Behaviour was read at published parameters from twelve starts, so the census undercounts kinds of behaviour.
  • The loop filters are proved for amplifying loops only; applied to braking loops, they remove the brakes of 10 clocks.
  • Loops written as formulas rather than reactions are invisible to it (13 models), and 102 loop searches stopped at their step limit.
  • BioModels holds what people chose to model and publish, not a random sample of biology.

9return

So, do real circuits fit?

As far as this census can see, mostly. Go back to January 2000: one sign separated the clock from the switch, and that sign is what the table counts. None of the 432 models that run without outside input broke the rules behind it; what’s left is an empty band where running totals should be, and a clock that moves when you redraw it. Chemistry’s table earned its keep when gallium turned up as predicted. This table’s gallium is still out there: a switch that keeps a running total, somewhere a cell has to decide how long a signal has lasted.

All of it is open: the code, the data and the supplements live in one repository, and sh analysis/run_all.sh 8 rebuilds every number on this page from the raw download in about two hours on a laptop. The repository is at github.com/kat-titter/every-line-is-a-loop. Change a definition, rerun, and see where your favourite circuit lands.

B. How the census works and how it was checked

Chemistry makes loops that regulate nothing. Binding and unbinding go out and back, like a two-way street, not round, like a roundabout. The filters of Baudier, Fages and Soliman [24], built on Soliman’s condition [34], remove them. They proved that dropping these loops loses no amplifying loop that could hold two states; the proof covers amplifying loops only (Supplement D).

Validation. Toy models with known answers are counted on every build, and Tyson, Chen and Novák’s seven textbook circuits [17] serve as an answer key. On the same release, Baudier, Fages and Soliman found no braking loop in 14% of models; this census finds none in 13%. An audit of the first full run found a backtracking bug that cost 42 of 106 rate-rule models some loops, and after the fix the small-model count fell from 179 to 175. Two independent runs from the raw download produce identical files.

C. More results: loop counts, behaviour, detail

Sorted by six behaviours and their mixtures, one loop gives 6 kinds, two loops 9 and three loops 6; merged into four behaviours, 4, 5 and 4. Neither two nor three loops shows more kinds of behaviour than it has elements, so by the essay’s own terms the rule survives. One loop shows more than its 4, though the essay set its test at two and three loops, and merged into four behaviours it gives exactly 4. The fruit fly’s clock models: Goldbeter’s 1995 model has one loop (−); Tyson and colleagues’ 1999 model has two (+ −); Leloup and Goldbeter’s 1998 and 1999 models have two each (− −); and their 1999 model with Gonze has 14. The paired running-total reading finds six models with a total kept by two molecules; one, a folate pathway, has it on a loop.

D. Methods: every step, setting and equation

Every step is a script in the analysis folder beside this page, and every setting that can move a number is given here. The data flow top to bottom:

Models into equations

BioModels release 31 (26 June 2017) holds 640 curated models, the set Baudier, Fages and Soliman used [24]. Each is read with libSBML [36], with function definitions expanded, and compiled by libRoadRunner [35]. The variables x are the species that change and are not fixed by an assignment rule, plus any quantity with a rate rule. With v(x) the reaction rates and N the stoichiometry matrix, whose entry Nkj is how many of molecule k reaction j makes (positive) or uses up (negative), each model is

dxdt=Nv(x)

plus the right-hand sides of its rate rules. 613 models could be read this way. Of the other 27, 5 use delay equations, 3 use logical rules, 6 have values roadrunner cannot set and 13 gave no arrows at the states read, among them Tyson’s mutual-inhibition switch, whose loop is hidden inside a formula.

Arrows

An arrow runs from molecule i to molecule k through reaction j when reaction j changes k and its rate depends on i. Its sign multiplies two signs: whether the rate rises or falls with i, and whether the reaction makes or uses up k.

σik(j)=sgn(∂vj∂xi)·sgn(Nkj)

The derivative is a central difference, with step h equal to a tenth of a percent of xi:

∂vj∂xi≈vj(x+hei)−vj(x−hei)2h

A difference smaller than 10−13 of the two rates counts as zero. Signs are read at five states of the model’s own run, t = 0, 1, 10, 100 and 1000, each reached from a fresh reset, so events and the clock apply only where they belong and one failed run loses one state, not all. A species sitting at exactly zero is lifted to 10−6 of the median level so its arrows can be read. An arrow seen at any of the five states counts, so one arrow can carry both signs. Rate rules give arrows the same way, from the derivative of their right-hand side.

Self-loops need care, because every molecule that decays has a trivial braking loop on itself. Decay, consumption and reverse flux are not counted. A braking self-loop is kept only when a reaction that makes the molecule slows as it rises, and its rate law stays non-negative when the molecule is pushed a thousand times higher (end-product inhibition, autorepression); a symbolic test with sympy looks for a positive root of the rate law’s numerator. An amplifying self-loop is dropped when it only comes from a substrate written as a total minus the molecule itself.

As a check, signs were also read at random states, each variable drawn log-uniformly between the lowest and highest levels it reaches on its own run to t = 1000. It gives 4 of 9 elements at two loops and 5 of 16 at three, and the two proven rules still hold.

Loops

A loop is an elementary circuit of the arrow graph, with each arc labelled by its reaction. Its sign is the product of its arcs’ signs, so it brakes when it holds an odd number of brakes:

sign(L)=∏a∈Lσa

Baudier, Fages and Soliman proved that three kinds of loop can be dropped without losing any amplifying loop that could hold two states [24]. The proofs run through the Jacobian; in plain terms, with the intuition for each:

  • A loop that uses two arrows from the same reaction, or from reactions that are the same up to a positive factor (stoichiometry columns that are positive multiples of each other). One reaction cannot amplify itself.
  • A loop that uses a reaction and its reverse (columns that are negative multiples). Out and back is not a loop.
  • A loop whose molecules make up a whole conserved pool, a weighting p ≥ 0 with pTN = 0 that no reaction changes. Moving material round a closed pool pushes nothing.

The proofs cover amplifying loops only. There is no matching theorem for braking loops, so applying the filters to them is a choice, and the alternative split below counts braking loops as drawn. A fourth test, the rank condition from the same paper, keeps a loop only if its reactions are independent, so that their stoichiometry columns cannot be combined to cancel; it drops cycles that just move material round:

rankN[:,R(L)]=|L|

Loops are listed by depth-first search from each molecule s through higher-numbered molecules in s’s strongly connected component, refusing an arc the moment it would break a filter. A search stops after 5,000,000 steps or 20,000 loops, and the model then counts as “search unfinished”. Counting steps instead of seconds is what makes reruns identical. Whether any amplifying loop survives the filters is decided separately by their vertex-elimination algorithm, which finishes even when listing doesn’t.

Each model gets three counts: c loops, k of them braking, j of them through a running total. The essay counts independent loops instead, the cyclomatic number of the union of all loops,

ν=E−V+C

with E arrows, V molecules and C connected pieces. It differs from c in 1 of the 175 small models. Sontag’s distance to positive feedback [37], the fewest sign flips that make every loop amplifying, gives a braking count that doesn’t depend on which loops are listed. Computed on the filtered loops, it differs from k in 17 of the 175.

Running totals

Strict reading. Molecule i keeps a running total if its own name appears in none of the rate laws that change it, so its rate cannot depend on its level:

∂fi∂xi≡0

Loose reading. Molecule i nearly keeps one if its own level moves its net rate by less than a tenth of the flow into it, at the last state reached (first-order decay scores exactly 1 at steady state):

ri=|∂fi/∂xi||xi|Φi<0.1 Φi=∑jmax(Nijvj,0)

Paired reading. Two molecules a and b keep one together if a weighted sum z of them changes at a rate that depends on neither, nor on any molecule P that shares a conserved total with them, but does depend on something else:

z=caxa+cbxb,∂z˙∂xq=0forq∈S∪P

A paired running total is two molecules whose weighted sum changes at a rate that depends on neither of them. The code screens for candidates numerically, then confirms each one exactly from the rate laws. Candidates come from the Jacobian J = N ∂v/∂x at the five states. The block J[S, S ∪ P] for S = {a, b}, stacked across states, must have rank one (smallest singular value under 10−4 of the largest, rows normalised). Sums equal to one molecule plus a constant once conserved totals are used are dropped. Each survivor is then confirmed from the rate laws: the weights are rounded to a ratio of whole numbers up to 12, sympy differentiates the weighted rate exactly, and the derivative must cancel to 10−9 at four random states spread over six orders of magnitude, with parameters at the model’s values. 31 candidates whose rate laws use conditional formulas could not be confirmed and are left out.

Behaviour

Each model runs at its published rates from its own start and eleven others. The others keep every conserved total of the start: they are corners of the set of states x ≥ 0 reachable as x0 + Nξ and capped at ten times the largest starting level, found by linear programs with random objectives, then mixed with Dirichlet(0.3) weights. The solver is CVODE [38] with relative tolerance 10−8 and absolute tolerance 10−12. Each run tries horizons of 102, 103, 104 and 105 time units, 2,001 samples each, and stops at the first that decides:

432 models run on their own; 11 small models never settle under these rules. Finding two states from twelve starts at one setting of the rates is a lower bound.

The proven rules

Two of the table’s rules are theorems, so the census uses them to check itself.

Two states need an amplifying loop. Soulé proved it [6]: if a system of smooth rate equations has two separate steady states, then at some state in between, the arrows the census draws (the signed Jacobian) contain an amplifying loop. The idea, roughly: between two stable states lies an unstable one, a hilltop, and a hilltop needs a disturbance that feeds back on itself and grows, which is an amplifying loop. Baudier, Fages and Soliman sharpened the statement for reaction networks: the loop can be taken to be one that survives their filters [24]. The census checks this with their elimination algorithm, which decides whether such a loop exists without listing loops.

A sustained rhythm needs a braking loop. Snoussi and Gouzé proved that a stable oscillation needs a braking loop through at least two molecules [7, 8]. The idea: to swing, a system must overshoot and turn back, and turning back means some chain of arrows eventually pushes the other way, which multiplies to a brake. The census checks it exactly: a strongly connected piece of the graph has no braking loop just when its molecules split into two camps so that every arrow’s sign is the product of its ends’ camps,

σab=sasb,sa∈{+1,−1}

which a single pass of two-colouring decides. Braking self-loops are left out of this check, as the theorem requires.

What the theorems do not say: they are necessary conditions, not sufficient ones. An amplifying loop does not guarantee two states, and a braking loop does not guarantee a rhythm. They also speak of the signs at some state of the system, which is why the census reads signs where each model actually runs, and again at random states as a check.

Counting elements

A model with c = ν loops goes in element (ν, k, j), and row ν has (ν + 1)2 elements. Grouping them like orbitals gives each one an address, with layers of 2l + 1:

n=ν+1,l=max(k,j),m=k−j (ν+1)2=∑l=0ν(2l+1)

Two other counts treat each loop as carrying its own type. If every loop is one of four types (amplifying or braking, with or without a running total), a row is a multiset of ν types: 4, 10 and 20 elements, of which the models fill 5 at two loops and 5 at three. If only braking loops can keep a running total, three types give 3, 6 and 10 elements, of which they fill 4 and 5. Counting amplifying loops after the filters and braking loops as drawn, the split the proofs support, moves 85 models to another cell; every sign pattern stays filled, and no clock is left without a brake. Kinds of behaviour are the distinct sets of labels among the models of a row that run on their own.

Replication

Baudier, Fages and Soliman ran the same three filters on the same release:

Amplifying loops differ in a direction sign reading can’t explain, because reading every setting at once, as they did, can only add arrows. The gap more likely comes from how each pipeline turns a model file into reactions; theirs rebuilds some models from their equations. Their headline, 160 of 506, adds a rewiring test not run here. Their case study, the ERK cascade of model 270, comes out as they found it: 18 loops survive the filters, all braking, so it cannot hold two states.
