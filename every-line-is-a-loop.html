Every line is a loop

A periodic table for biology

Kat Titterton, October 2026. Draft for comment.

In 1869 Mendeleev left blank squares in his table and said what would fill them. Gallium turned up six years later, close to the density he had written in the blank. Biology has never had a table that could do that. The question here is why not, and whether the missing ingredient is only that we have been counting the wrong thing.

Chemistry has a periodic table because electrons come in integers. Biology's integers are loops: closed paths whose number, sign and nesting can be counted where sequence cannot. Sort loops by those numbers and a table appears that widens like Mendeleev's, with one element that never closes. That element is aging, and followed far enough it is a loop too. Five figures carry the argument; the text says only what they cannot. The payoff is practical: a finite list of hypotheses, one assay that tests them without a parts list, and one vocabulary for cancer, autoimmunity and neurodegeneration.

Five words. A loop is a closed chain of influence, A acts on B acts on A. Its sign is positive if the chain amplifies and negative if it opposes. Its period is how long one trip round takes. An integrator is a loop that feeds back the running total of an error rather than the error itself. A generator is the simplest machine that could produce a given signal.

Biology's integers are loops

Chemistry got its table because electrons fill shells in whole numbers; Mendeleev's grid is that counting rule made visible. Biology looks continuous almost everywhere, except in one place: topology. A closed loop carries integers that a sequence never does. Cut nothing, and the linking number of a closed DNA domain is fixed; it splits into twist and writhe [1]:

Lk=Tw+Wr

Lk, linking number: how many times the two strands pass through each other. A whole number, fixed until a strand is cut.

Tw, twist: how the strands wind around the axis of the helix. Continuous.

Wr, writhe: how the axis coils over itself. Continuous, and traded against twist, which is why a plasmid can look twisted here and coiled there.

Mendeleev sorted by atomic weight and mostly got the order right, with inversions like tellurium before iodine, until Moseley found atomic number underneath. Gene sequence is biology's atomic weight. Loop context is its atomic number. The inversions are real: at the EPHA4 locus, structural variants that leave every gene intact move a domain boundary, a cluster of limb enhancers lands on the wrong promoter, and a limb comes out malformed [2].

So take loops as the elements, ask what numbers they carry, and let the shape of the table follow.

Sign is valence

If loops are the elements, what plays the part of valence, the property that decides how an element behaves? The sign. Thomas conjectured in 1981 that memory, meaning more than one stable state, needs a positive loop, and that a clock, meaning sustained oscillation, needs a negative one. The first rule was proved in general by Soulé in 2003 [3]; the second, for negative loops of length two or more, by Snoussi and by Gouzé in 1998 [4]. A third class comes from integral action, a loop that feeds back the accumulated error rather than the current one. It returns exactly to baseline under a sustained input, and chemotaxis in E. coli is built on it [5].

All three classes are readable from a time series alone, with no parts list.

Rows are loop count; period is mass

Then what are the rows? A periodic table needs integer axes; continuous quantities belong inside the cells. Biology's integers are the number of independent loops in a circuit (ν), how many of them are negative (k), and how many integrate rather than respond (j). Period is continuous, so it goes in the cell, the way atomic mass does. That gives isotopes: a positive loop at milliseconds (a riboswitch) and at hours (the lac operon) are the same element at different rates, and KaiABC, three proteins with a 24-hour cycle that runs in a test tube [6], is a heavy isotope of the single negative loop. The environment's real periods, the day, the month, the year, decide which isotopes are abundant, not where the cells sit; chemistry separates its table from nucleosynthesis the same way.

Row ν holds (ν+1)² elements, and the formula is just counting: each of the ν loops is positive or negative, so the number of negative ones runs from 0 to ν, which is ν+1 choices; each loop also either integrates or not, another ν+1 choices; multiply. That is n² with n = ν+1, one factor of two short of chemistry's 2n², which I take as a sign that nesting order is the missing binary. Element zero, the integrator with no loop at all, is the one entry that never returns.

The + + + cell was blank in the first draft, and the blank was my ignorance, not nature's. OCT4, SOX2 and NANOG each bind their own gene and each other's, a fully interconnected positive circuit [25]. The table says what such an element must do: positive feedback on a monotone system yields multiple stable states with hysteresis and cannot oscillate [26], so the occupant should be a memory with more than two settings and no clock. That is a stem cell's job description. The same reading fills the other two blanks in row 3.

+ + −. Two positive loops can hold a state and one negative loop can end it. The receptor for epidermal growth factor, EGFR, is wired this way. It switches itself on; it and PTPRG, an enzyme that strips its activating tags, switch each other off; and a second such enzyme, PTPN2, closes a negative loop that sets how long the signal lasts [27]. The state it holds is a working memory: a cell keeps pointing toward where a growth signal was for about forty minutes after the signal is gone, and can still turn toward a new one [28].

− − −. With no positive loop there is only one state to be in [3], so three negative loops can hold a level or keep a beat but cannot remember. NF-κB, the factor that turns on inflammation genes, makes its own three brakes, IκBα, IκBε and A20 [29, 30]. It pulses in and out of the nucleus, and after each pulse it needs 100 to 200 minutes before it can answer in full again [29].

One caveat belongs on the figure. Rates can cross a bifurcation: a negative loop with too little delay sits still instead of ticking. An element is a circuit plus its regime, and Thomas's rules are necessary, not sufficient.

The blanks are the point. A table earns the name by its gaps, and this one has two kinds: cells that should fill with search, and a single entry the closure logic cannot reach.

Loops are models

Why would nature count loops at all? Because loops hold information, and they hold it because loops are models. Conant and Ashby proved in 1970 that a regulator which is maximally successful and simple must be isomorphic to the system it regulates [7]. Francis and Wonham made the linear case rigorous in 1976: a controller that robustly cancels a class of disturbances contains a copy of their generator, the simplest machine that could have produced them [8]. Sontag extended the result to adaptation with signal detection, and named circadian clocks as candidate internal models of the day [9]. Integral feedback is the model of a constant input, which is why chemotaxis adapts exactly [5]. An entrained oscillator is a copy of the planet's rotation.

This derives the table instead of sorting into it. Rows are periods because the environment's spectrum is discrete: molecular, tidal, daily, lunar, annual, generational. Columns are the generators a loop can copy: a constant, a pair of states, a sinusoid. Nesting is nested models, a day inside a year.

The open column follows from the same principle, with one detail worth stating exactly. Sontag's theorem assumes the generating system is Poisson-stable, meaning its trajectories keep returning near where they began [9]. Every periodic signal qualifies. A monotonic signal does not, and its only generators are integrators, which fill without bound. A bounded cell can carry a bounded copy of every recurrent feature of its world, while its copy of time can only be a counter that never resets. Read this way, the epigenetic clock is not damage. It is the internal model of time's arrow, with the one property such a model must have: it does not come back.

Every line is a loop

What about the one entry that never closes? A line is a loop you have not followed far enough. Every isotope of element zero in Figure 2 is a loop seen from inside its own period: differentiation is an arc of development, aging an arc of the germline. Line those isotopes up by period and each hands off to a closed loop one period longer, which strings the periods into a helix. That is the shape de Chancourtois gave the first periodic system in 1862, a helix wound on a cylinder, seven years before Mendeleev cut it into rows [10].

The helix bites its tail once. Reproduction rejoins the last row to the first, and the reset is measurable: epigenetic age falls to a minimum around gastrulation, the ground zero from which aging is then counted [11]. Partial reprogramming with OSK restores youthful methylation and vision in old mice [12], a chord drawn across the same circle. The germline is the loop and the soma is the arc that does not come back, which is Kirkwood's disposable soma restated as topology [13].

One closure is missing. Lineages never reset, because no generator exists for the gene pool, so evolution stays a line threaded through the hole of the torus. Individuals loop; lineages do not. Biology closes the arrow of time for organisms only, which may be why aging feels programmed from inside and random from above.

Three diseases, one algebra

In loop language a disease is a loop whose generator no longer matches the world, or a loop that has opened. Three of the big ones are the same failure in three tissues: a switch held on while its negative loop is deleted, so a process that should close runs as a counter.

Cancer. Tissue growth is a switch held by negative loops: tumour suppressors, contact inhibition, apoptosis, senescence. Two of the hallmarks of cancer are exactly the switch held on and the loops deleted, sustaining proliferative signalling and evading growth suppressors [17]. The decisive experiment exists. Restoring p53, one negative loop, in established mouse tumours caused regression, by apoptosis in lymphomas and by senescence in sarcomas and liver carcinomas [18, 19]. Close the loop and the counter stops.

Autoimmunity. Immune memory is a switch; regulatory T cells and checkpoints are its negative loops. Delete the loop genetically and you get IPEX, where FOXP3 mutations remove regulatory T cells and cause fatal multi-organ autoimmunity [20]. Delete it on purpose and you get the same thing with a different name: checkpoint blockade treats cancer by removing the brakes on T cells, and its immune-related adverse events are autoimmunity in the same patient [21]. Cancer and autoimmunity are one loop with the sign of the intervention flipped.

Neurodegeneration. Seeded aggregation is a positive loop: a misfolded seed converts healthy protein into more seeds [22]. Clearance is the negative loop that should close it. Dividing cells also have a reset: yeast mother cells keep the damaged proteins and daughters are born clean [23]. Neurons never divide, so they have no reset and no dilution by growth; when clearance fails, the aggregation switch runs as a counter for the organism's whole life. That is why these are diseases of late life: their clock is the aging counter itself.

None of this is new biology. What the table adds is a prediction across all three: the lost loop should appear as a missing resonance in a frequency sweep before pathology does, and the same two interventions, close the negative loop or impose a reset, should work in each.

What this gives an agent doing science

An automated lab needs three things a molecular parts list does not give it: a finite hypothesis space, an assay it can run without hand-built reagents, and rules for throwing models away before fitting them. The table supplies all three.

  1. A hypothesis space it can enumerate. A cell's loop content is a point in (ν, k, j) plus a regime, a few hundred cases at most, instead of an open-ended network of molecules.
  2. An assay with no parts list. A frequency sweep: periodic input, label-free time series, fit the transfer function, read off the generators. It needs a pump and a camera, not an antibody, so an agent can run it closed-loop and overnight.
  3. Rejection rules. Thomas's conditions, the nesting rule that no clock runs faster than its sub-loops, and the (ν+1)² bound all discard candidate models before a single parameter is fit.
  4. Borrowed tools. Loop content is system identification, so sixty years of control-theory code applies unchanged.
  5. A different objective. The intervention search runs over loop-closing moves, restore a negative loop, entrain a clock, impose a reset, rather than over parts to inhibit.
  6. A provenance ledger. Every claim here is tagged theorem, derivation or pattern, which is the weighting an agent needs to decide what to trust.

Experiments that would settle it

Six, each with the result that would kill it.

  1. Count the elements. Curate the two- and three-loop circuits in a model database, classify each by dynamical class, and count the classes; mining BioModels for circuit structure has been done for Thomas's positive-loop condition [24]. Prediction: 9 and 16. More kills the rule; fewer names the quantum number that is dead weight.
  2. Sweep a cell. Drive cells with periodic osmotic or nutrient input in a microfluidic device, record a label-free time series, fit the frequency response, as was done for the yeast osmo-adaptation pathway [14, 15]. Prediction: the fitted generators land in the table's cells, and the rows are discrete. Resonances that fall between cells, with no circuit behind them, kill the integer axis.
  3. Hunt the forbidden element. Search the same databases for a multi-loop oscillator faster than its own sub-loops. One verified instance kills the nesting rule.
  4. Dig for a niche fossil. Compare the frequency response of lineages evolved for thousands of generations in constant conditions with their ancestors. Prediction: one specific resonance gone, the loop that modelled the feature held constant. General decay with no specific loss kills the model-of-the-world reading.
  5. Watch the reset. Track epigenetic age at single-cell resolution through gastrulation and through partial reprogramming. Prediction: a counter emptied, a monotone fall to a floor, rather than a loop restored, which would oscillate or overshoot.
  6. Find the loop before the disease. In models of each disease in Figure 5, sweep before pathology appears. Prediction: the negative loop is already missing from the response. Then close it, as p53 restoration did [18, 19], and the counter should stop. A disease whose sweep looks normal until the end kills the mismatch reading for that disease.

What it does not do

The claims above are not equally secure. Weigh the table with this gradient in mind.

Three holes are visible from here. Space is missing: a Turing pattern is a clock with a wavelength, and the segmentation clock converts time into space, so the real table has period and wavelength as its rows. Mechanism is missing by design: the table says which generators a cell carries, never which molecules carry them, as Mendeleev's grid said nothing about bonds. And the counting rule, the one thing that would turn this from a lens into a foundation, is derived but untested, and it leaves nesting order out.
